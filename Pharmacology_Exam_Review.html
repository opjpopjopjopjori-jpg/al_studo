1 · Antihypertensive Drugs

Lecture 1 · pp. 1–12

Blood pressure and core choices

The lecture defines hypertension as sustained systolic BP >140 mmHg or diastolic BP >90 mmHg. Lifestyle measures include sodium restriction, weight reduction, physical activity and smoking cessation. The five major drug classes are ACE inhibitors, ARBs, β-blockers, calcium-channel blockers (CCBs) and thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics.

Renin–angiotensin system

ACE inhibitors (enalapril, captopril, lisinopril) block angiotensin I → II and reduce bradykinin breakdown. Less angiotensin II reduces vasoconstriction and aldosterone; more bradykinin promotes nitric oxide and prostacyclin. They reduce preload and afterload without reflex tachycardia, reduce albuminuria and slow diabetic and non-diabetic CKD progression. Important in HFrEF and after MI. Adverse effects: dry cough (~10%), hypotension, hyperkalemia, renal impairment and rare angioedema. Avoid in pregnancy and severe bilateral renal artery stenosis; monitor potassium and renal function.
ARBs (e.g., losartan, valsartan) block AT₁ receptors and have similar cardiovascular and renal effects, but do not raise bradykinin; cough and angioedema are less frequent. They are fetotoxic. Do not routinely combine an ACE inhibitor and an ARB for hypertension: no added outcome benefit and more renal adverse events.
Aliskiren directly inhibits renin, acting earlier in the cascade; avoid in pregnancy and do not routinely combine it with an ACE inhibitor or ARB. Diarrhea, cough and angioedema may occur.

Pharmacokinetics: The hypertension lecture identifies captopril and lisinopril as active without hepatic conversion; fosinopril is not primarily eliminated by the kidneys; enalaprilat is available IV.

Calcium-channel and adrenergic drugs

CCBs reduce calcium entry and predominantly dilate arterioles. Dihydropyridines (e.g., amlodipine, nifedipine) are more vascular-selective. Verapamil and diltiazem also slow cardiac conduction; verapamil commonly causes constipation and may cause AV block. Avoid verapamil in heart failure or AV block. High doses of short-acting CCBs can cause excessive vasodilation, reflex cardiac stimulation and increased MI risk.

β-blockers lower cardiac output and inhibit renin release. Metoprolol and atenolol are β₁-selective; propranolol is nonselective. Labetalol and carvedilol block α₁, β₁ and β₂ receptors. Benefits are especially relevant with angina, prior MI, tachyarrhythmia or chronic HF. Nonselective agents can worsen asthma; use caution with heart block and acute HF. Adverse effects include bradycardia and fatigue. Taper rather than stop abruptly to avoid rebound angina or MI.

α₁ blockers (prazosin, doxazosin, terazosin) relax arteries and veins; postural hypotension, initial reflex tachycardia and salt/water retention limit use as initial antihypertensives. Prostate-selective α₁ blockers can treat benign prostatic hyperplasia.

Clonidine is a central α₂ agonist for resistant hypertension; sedation, dry mouth and constipation are important. Abrupt withdrawal can cause rebound hypertension. Methyldopa, also centrally acting, has a record of safety in pregnancy; labetalol and hydralazine are also discussed for pregnancy-related hypertension. ACE inhibitors and ARBs are contraindicated.

Hydralazine and minoxidil directly relax arterioles. Reflex tachycardia and fluid retention can be countered with a β-blocker and diuretic. Hydralazine may cause a reversible lupus-like syndrome; minoxidil causes hypertrichosis.

Hypertensive emergency: severe BP elevation with impending or progressive target-organ damage requires timely IV treatment. The lecture lists IV agents including nicardipine, nitroprusside and labetalol. Resistant hypertension remains above goal despite an optimal three-drug regimen including a diuretic.

2 · Antianginal Drugs

Lecture 2 · pp. 1–10

Angina results from an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand. Reducing heart rate, contractility and afterload or relieving coronary spasm improves this balance.

β-blockers: β₁ blockade lowers heart rate, contractility and oxygen demand and improves exercise tolerance in effort angina. Avoid agents with intrinsic sympathomimetic activity in angina or after MI. They can worsen vasospastic angina. Avoid abrupt withdrawal; nonselective agents can worsen asthma.
CCBs: arterial dilation reduces afterload; coronary dilation relieves spasm. Amlodipine acts mainly on vessels. Verapamil and diltiazem slow AV conduction; avoid non-dihydropyridines in depressed cardiac function or conduction abnormalities. Short-acting dihydropyridines are avoided in CAD.
Organic nitrates: nitrate → nitric oxide → guanylate cyclase → ↑cGMP → vascular smooth-muscle relaxation. Predominant venodilation reduces preload; coronary dilation also contributes. Sublingual nitroglycerin provides rapid relief during an acute attack. Headache, flushing, postural hypotension and reflex tachycardia are important. PDE-5 inhibitors such as sildenafil are contraindicated because severe hypotension can result. A daily 10–12-hour nitrate-free interval reduces tolerance.

Nicorandil combines potassium-channel activation and a nitrate group, producing arterial and venous dilation. Ranolazine inhibits late sodium current and reduces intracellular sodium/calcium overload in chronic angina. Trimetazidine inhibits fatty-acid β-oxidation, promoting glucose oxidation during ischemia. Ivabradine inhibits the SA-node If current and lowers heart rate without a negative inotropic effect.

3 · Heart Failure

Lecture 3+4 · pp. 1–14

HF involves inadequate cardiac output; symptoms include dyspnea, fatigue and fluid retention. HFrEF has reduced systolic pumping; diastolic failure involves impaired relaxation and filling. Sympathetic and RAAS activation initially compensate but increase workload, fluid retention and remodeling. Treatment aims to relieve symptoms, slow progression and improve survival.

ACE inhibitors / ARBs: decrease preload and afterload; ACE inhibitors improve survival in HFrEF. ARBs substitute when ACE inhibitors are not tolerated. Monitor potassium and renal function; avoid in pregnancy.
Evidence-supported β-blockers: bisoprolol, carvedilol and extended-release metoprolol succinate decrease harmful chronic sympathetic effects and support reverse remodeling. Start low and titrate after stabilization.
Aldosterone antagonists: spironolactone and eplerenone counter aldosterone; spironolactone reduces remodeling and HF mortality. Watch for hyperkalemia. Spironolactone can cause gynecomastia and menstrual irregularities.
Diuretics: reduce plasma volume, preload, congestion and edema. Loop diuretics are commonly used when extensive diuresis is needed, including with renal insufficiency. Excessive diuresis can cause hypovolemia.
Sacubitril/valsartan: an ARB plus neprilysin inhibitor that increases natriuretic peptide activity and reduces preload, afterload and fibrosis. Hypotension and angioedema can occur. Stop an ACE inhibitor at least 36 hours before starting this combination.

Ivabradine: inhibits SA-node If current, slowing heart rate without reducing contractility. The lecture describes use in symptomatic HFrEF in sinus rhythm, with HR above 70 bpm despite optimized therapy including a tolerated β-blocker or where β-blockers are contraindicated. It is not a rate-control drug for atrial fibrillation; bradycardia and luminous visual phenomena can occur.

Hydralazine + isosorbide dinitrate: arterial dilation lowers afterload and venodilation lowers preload. Headache, hypotension and tachycardia can occur.

Digoxin: inhibits Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → increases intracellular Na⁺ → reduces Ca²⁺ extrusion → increases intracellular Ca²⁺ and contractility. Enhanced vagal tone slows AV conduction. Its narrow therapeutic index makes toxicity important: nausea, vomiting, visual changes and arrhythmias. It is mainly renally eliminated. Hypokalemia can predispose patients taking digoxin to ventricular arrhythmias (diuretics lecture, p. 9).

Acute inotropes: dobutamine increases cAMP and calcium entry and is administered by IV infusion in hospital for acute HF. Milrinone inhibits phosphodiesterase, increasing cAMP; long-term therapy may increase mortality. Nesiritide is recombinant BNP given IV; hypotension and worsening renal function may occur.

Questions and answer keys are transcribed from the supplied question files; selected for lecture relevance. Question numbers follow the source files. Some screenshot-based questions are included where the supplied text contains the complete question and options.

Antihypertensives

Q1. Dry cough is a recognized side effect of which class of antihypertensive drugs, occurring in approximately 10% of patients?

A. ACE inhibitors B. Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs) C. Quinidine D. Beta-blockers E. Alpha-1 blockers

Answer: A. ACE inhibitors

Q2. ACE inhibitors and ARBs should NOT be combined for the treatment of hypertension because:

A. Combination is only indicated in case of angina B. They interact chemically with each other C. Their action is much more than needed D. There is no added benefit from using them together E. Their use together should be included with diuretics

Answer: D. There is no added benefit from using them together

Q4. High doses of short-acting calcium-channel blockers (e.g., short-acting nifedipine) should be avoided in hypertensive patients because:

A. They increase the risk of myocardial infarction due to excessive vasodilation and marked reflex cardiac stimulation B. They decrease the risk of myocardial infarction due to excessive vasoconstriction C. They are recommended for first-line treatment of angina D. They are specifically recommended in diabetic hypertensive patients

Answer: A. They increase the risk of myocardial infarction due to excessive vasodilation and marked reflex cardiac stimulation

Q7. Which antihypertensive drug class is strictly contraindicated in pregnant women due to fetotoxicity?

A. Methyldopa B. Valsartan (ARBs / ACEIs) C. Nifedipine D. Hydralazine E. Labetalol

Answer: B. Valsartan (ARBs / ACEIs)

Q9. Concerning selective alpha-1 adrenoceptor blockers (e.g., Prazosin, Doxazosin, Terazosin), all of the following statements are false EXCEPT:

A. alpha-blockers are recommended as initial first-line monotherapy for hypertension B. alpha-1 blockers with greater selectivity for prostate smooth muscle are used in the treatment of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) C. They increase peripheral vascular resistance and elevate arterial blood pressure D. Long-term persistent tachycardia is the main side effect E. Salt and water retention does not occur

Answer: B. alpha-1 blockers with greater selectivity for prostate smooth muscle are used in the treatment of benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)

Q10. Direct-acting arteriolar vasodilators such as Minoxidil produce which characteristic adverse effect?

A. Hypertrichosis (excessive growth of body hair) B. Alopecia C. Hypokalemia D. Gingival hyperplasia

Answer: A. Hypertrichosis (excessive growth of body hair)

Q11. Direct-acting arterial vasodilators (such as Hydralazine) are almost always co-administered with a Beta-blocker and a Diuretic because:

A. Beta-blockers control reflex tachycardia and diuretics prevent sodium and water retention B. Hydralazine causes severe bradycardia and hyperkalemia C. Hydralazine inhibits cardiac contractility D. Combining them increases the hepatic clearance of hydralazine

Answer: A. Beta-blockers control reflex tachycardia and diuretics prevent sodium and water retention

Q12. Which drug is a direct renin inhibitor that acts early in the RAAS pathway to prevent the conversion of angiotensinogen to angiotensin I?

A. Aliskiren B. Losartan C. Enalapril D. Eplerenone

Answer: A. Aliskiren

Q13. ACE inhibitors lower blood pressure primarily by inhibiting angiotensin II formation, which leads to:

A. Decreased secretion of aldosterone, resulting in decreased sodium and water retention B. Increased secretion of aldosterone C. Increased cardiac output and heart rate D. Increased total peripheral resistance

Answer: A. Decreased secretion of aldosterone, resulting in decreased sodium and water retention

Q14. Endothelial nitric oxide and prostacyclin act on vascular smooth muscle as:

A. Potent vasodilators B. Potent vasoconstrictors C. Potent diuretics D. Weak vasodilators

Answer: A. Potent vasodilators

Antianginal Drugs

Q15. In the management of angina pectoris, Beta-adrenergic blockers decrease myocardial oxygen demand by blocking beta-1 receptors, resulting in:

A. Decreased heart rate B. Decreased myocardial contractility C. Decreased cardiac output D. Decreased blood pressure E. All of the above

Answer: E. All of the above

Q16. The fundamental mechanism of action of organic nitrates (e.g., Nitroglycerin) in relieving angina involves:

A. Conversion to nitric oxide (NO) which increases cellular cGMP and relaxes vascular smooth muscle (EDRF-like action) B. Direct stimulation of myocardial beta-1 receptors C. Direct inhibition of Na+/K+-ATPase D. Selective blockade of cardiac calcium channels

Answer: A. Conversion to nitric oxide (NO) which increases cellular cGMP and relaxes vascular smooth muscle (EDRF-like action)

Q19. Concomitant administration of organic Nitrates with PDE-5 inhibitors (e.g., Sildenafil) is strictly contraindicated because:

Q20. Tolerance to the antianginal actions of organic nitrates can be prevented or overcome by:

Q21. Which antianginal agent belongs to the class of Potassium Channel Activators, exhibiting both arterial vasodilation (via K+ channel opening) and venous dilation (via a nitrate moiety)?

Q28. Which antihypertensive / antianginal drug class should be avoided or used with extreme caution in hypertensive patients with co-existing bronchial asthma?
